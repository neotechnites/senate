## What the research actually supports (short)

- YouTube's own retention report defines "Intro" as the share still watching at 30 seconds, says a high figure means the opening matched title/thumbnail and held interest, and tells you to revise the first 30 seconds if it is low. Its "Top moments" guidance: if the moments where nobody leaves occur late, move that content earlier, because the audience shrinks over the video (YouTube Help, key moments).
- Nobody credible publishes an "8-10 second re-hook" rule. The intervals in marketing blogs range from 8 s to 90 s with no data. The leaked MrBeast production doc, as reported, says the first minute is where most viewers leave ("we lost 21 million viewers in the first minute"), the thumbnail promise must be delivered, and "no dull moments" (Creator Handbook, Passionfruit). MrBeast via Colin and Samir: first seconds match the thumbnail, subtitles, highly visual, because many watch with sound off. Colin Rosenblum calls YouTube a "hover and hook" platform (autoplay preview). Treat "re-hook" as: every segment ends on something new.
- George Blackman (scriptwriter; publishes retention graphs): a drop comes when "I'd make a point, THEN talk about it for ages", viewers skip ahead to the next payoff; a good hook gives concept and stakes in three sentences and reaches the meat at ~10 s; one CTA, delivered right after the final payoff; outros that run on after the payoff drop; "resolve one curiosity gap before opening another".
- Derek Lieu: order is Genre, Hook, Content; "the longer the trailer goes without showing a hook, the more it will seem like the game is a lesser version" of something else; "no matter how quickly you think you need to hook, you're probably still not doing it quickly enough"; ideally within 10-15 s (Lieu via GameDiscoverCo and Push To Talk; his own editing page).
- Paddy Galloway (via Colin and Samir show notes): "the difference between a million views and 28 million is how you package it"; specific numbers in titles create the curiosity gap (Ian Lauer: "10 minutes, 1 hour, 24 hours"); formats should be familiar in the niche with a twist.
- Chris Zukowski: devlogs mostly reach other developers, not players; Forge Industry's devlogs were "consistently the worst performing videos" the team uploaded; to make YouTube work you must be "a YouTuber who makes games", positioned as entertainer not educator; Jonas Tyroller says most Will You Snail wishlists came from YouTube but Thronefall's devlogs did not "make a noticeable dent" (howtomarketagame 2023, 2024, 2025). Consequence for this script: it is a YouTube video first; the parts that only interest developers (menu scaling, exporter) should be short and the parts that interest players (being watched, the face-plant corpse) long.
- YouTube's own creator playbook on subscribe asks: ask once, briefly, "don't over do it, and have a good reason for why they should". End screens occupy the last 5-20 s of the video (YouTube Reporting API docs). There is no official data on when to ask; forum and blog evidence conflicts. Across the seven comparables, CTA weight made no visible difference and nobody asked early.
- Comment asks: comment rate (comments/views) across the seven comparables is 0.18-0.44% regardless of whether there was an ask. Codeer asked nothing: 0.33%. Caleb asked mid-video and at the end: 0.32%. Dani asked one specific question (name the game): 0.43%, the highest of the big performers. Specific beats open-ended; an ask does not hurt.
- Chapters: YouTube publishes rules (0:00 first, at least three, each 10 s or longer) but no retention data; third-party "20% watch time" claims have no primary source. Low priority, free to add.
- Explaining a concept: Nova (best relative performer, 130x channel median) spends 38 s explaining what a factory game is "so even a little kid can understand"; Codeer 28 s on procedural animation. Both short, both with a picture. Works when it is one plain paragraph with a diagram and a button at the end.
- Personal stories: the strong comparables use personal material as stakes (unemployed, 17, never shown anyone) or as a 20-60 s anecdote with a punchline. None spends more than ~50 s on taste. Squidge's 40 s of taste before the game is flagged as the weak part of a weak performer. Nova's "why small games don't work for me" is 47 s.
- Length of devlog 0s that broke out: 13:26, 10:32, 7:27, 5:09 (strong); 4:17, 6:29, 6:03 (weak). The two longest did best relative to channel size, provided they had an arc.

## The changes, ranked by expected effect on views and retention

1. SPECIFIC PART: the whole first 3:07, where the game is not named or described until the bracketed premise -> THE CHANGE: say the premise in one sentence inside the hook, by 0:35, then keep the full pitch where it is. Rewrite the end of the hook: "...Because the game I want to make is online, it's multiplayer, and the part they'd really hate: I want it to have depth. One of you sits in a tower with a sniper rifle. Everyone else runs for the exit. Nobody down there is on your team. And I want that to be a game you play for months, not three hours and uninstall." -> WHY: YouTube's Intro metric is measured at 30 s and is about whether the opening pays off the title/thumbnail; Lieu's order is genre then hook, and "the longer it goes without the hook the more it looks like a lesser version of something else"; Blackman's model hook gives concept and stakes in three sentences and hits the meat by 10 s. Of the seven comparables, every one explains the game in a single sentence either in the first line or straight after the stakes; none waits past 2:00 except Nova (4:19), whose first four minutes are an argument with cutaways, not setup.

2. SPECIFIC PART: no game footage is indicated anywhere before "(shot of some really shitty map)" at ~5:40 -> THE CHANGE: open on the sniper scope view from the tower at second zero, one runner lapping, one clean kill, cut to black, then the voice starts; keep game or Halo Reach footage under every sentence of the first three minutes, no talking-head or black screen. -> WHY: MrBeast via Colin and Samir: first seconds must match the thumbnail, be highly visual, subtitled, because many hover with sound off; Colin Rosenblum: YouTube is a "hover and hook" platform; Lieu: open on gameplay before a viewer can click away. RujiK, Codeer and Dani all have the game on screen from 0:00. The transcripts research shows seconds-to-gameplay is not the discriminator for views, but it is for the sound-off autoplay preview that decides the click.

3. ## Titles (three options)

1. "I'm Making the Game Every Solo Dev Says Not To Make | Devlog 0" -> the claim is the dev's choice, which is the structure of the best relative performer (Nova, "Breaking the No.1 Rule in Solo Game Development | Devlog 0", 661 subs to 177k views, ~130x channel median). Keeps "Devlog 0" as the niche-familiar suffix (Galloway: familiar within the niche, plus a twist).
2. "Turning a Halo Reach Custom Game Into a Real Game | Devlog 0" -> a specific, searchable noun ("Halo Reach custom game") does the curiosity work that Galloway's Ian Lauer example credits to specificity; it reaches Halo players, not only devlog watchers (Zukowski's complaint about devlog audiences). Weakest if the thumbnail does not show the tower.
3. "One Sniper. Everyone Else Runs. My First Multiplayer Game (Devlog 0)" -> the premise as the claim (RujiK/Codeer pattern: premise in one line, flat); strongest match to a scope-view thumbnail, and it tells the sound-off scroller the genre before the hook, which is Lieu's order.
Avoid: "Introduction", "Devlog #0 - Introduction", or the word "panopticon" alone in the title; the transcripts show "Introduction" and "(The Right Way)" carried nothing, and "panopticon" is a word the audience has to be taught (the video does that; the title shouldn't need to).

## Thumbnails (three concepts)

1. Through the scope: the sniper's scope view from the tower, crosshair on one tiny runner mid-sprint between cover, pit walls of cells behind. Two words max: "ONE GUARD". The first second of the video is this exact shot. -> MrBeast via Colin and Samir: first seconds must match the thumbnail; Lieu: the hook (being watched) shown, not described.
2. The pit from above: the hell map seen from the tower top, a single red sightline to one runner, the rest in shadow. No text, or "YOU'RE BEING WATCHED". -> the one image that explains the panopticon without a word; the "familiar plus twist" Galloway rule (an arena map, but it's a prison and you're in the crosshair).
3. The face-plant: the corpse lying face down on the ring, your buddy's runner standing over it, caption "FIRST PLAYTEST". -> the Dani/Nova fail-cutaway tone in a still; it promises the failure beat, which is the Top moment of the script and the segment players (not developers) will share. Riskier for click-through on a zero-sub channel because it hides the premise; A/B it against 1.

## What not to change

The contrarian hook is the right structure: it is the exact shape of the best relative performer and the title can carry the same claim. The bracketed premise sentence is the cleanest thing in the script and matches the best premise statement among the comparables (Squidge: genre, inspirations, setting, one objective, 30 s); say it, don't rewrite it. The duck hunt origin and the "felt like you were being watched" line are the real reason this game exists and lead straight into the panopticon drop; keep every word. The "it all worked, first try" fake-out, the "these are the art skills I'm working with" cutaway and the deadpan under-sell are the tone every strong comparable shares. The playtest is a real failure beat told as a story with named bugs, which the weakest comparable (O and Co) lacked entirely. The open feedback question, the specific "next", a CTA only at the end, and an 11-13 minute runtime all match what worked. Nothing here needs more polish, more hype, or more "epic"; it needs the game on screen earlier, the Melee essay shorter, and one ask instead of three.
