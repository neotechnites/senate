# PANOPTICON — Steam Pricing
**Research brief · 2026-09-09 · question: what should PANOPTICON cost on Steam at 1.0 launch (target 2027-04-01)?**

Author decides. This brief presents options with tradeoffs and the evidence behind them. Every sourced claim carries its URL inline. **ESTIMATE** marks a number I derived rather than found. **NO SOURCE FOUND** marks something I looked for and could not source — I did not fill those with guesses. **INCOMPLETE** marks a section where the research run was cut short.

**Data provenance.** The comparables table and the price-distribution analysis in §2 were pulled live by me on 2026-09-09 from three public endpoints: Steam's store API (`store.steampowered.com/api/appdetails`), Steam's review API (`store.steampowered.com/appreviews`), and the SteamSpy API (`steamspy.com/api.php`). Those numbers are first-hand, not second-hand. Historical launch prices and price-change events came from Steam's own news/announcement feed and press coverage, cited per row. **SteamDB — the canonical price-history source — was Cloudflare-blocked (HTTP 403) on every attempt.** The Wayback Machine was heavily rate-limited (HTTP 429) but did work intermittently, and is the source of most of the launch prices in §1b. That is why several *historical launch prices* below remain NO SOURCE FOUND while every *current* price is solid.

## RECOMMENDATION

### The short answer

**$9.99, with a 20% launch discount ($7.99) for 7 days.**

The developer's instinct of $12 is directionally sensible and lands inside the plausible range. But **$12.99 is the one price in this neighbourhood that the evidence actively rules out** — not on taste, but because it is dominated: it converts like a $14.99 game and earns like a $9.99 one. That finding is below and it is the most actionable thing in this brief.

### The decisive calculation

There is exactly one sourced price discontinuity in Steam wishlist behaviour, and it sits at **$10**.

GameDiscoverCo, measuring across all Steam games rather than a survey: the median ratio of Week-1 sales to launch wishlist balance is **0.14–0.15×** for the all-price cohort, but drops to **0.10× for games priced over $10 at launch** — *"we know that very inexpensive games convert better… if we sort only for games that cost >$10 at launch, we see a median drop all the way down to 0.10x"* — [GameDiscoverCo, "The state of Steam wishlist 'conversions': 2024-2025", 2025-10-17](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-state-of-steam-wishlist-conversions).

Apply that to the three candidate prices. Week-1 units = wishlists × conversion; Year-1 gross = 3.16 × Week-1 gross ([GameDiscoverCo 2024-04-15](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/revealed-the-state-of-steam-wishlist)); net = 70%.

| Launch wishlists | | **$9.99 @ 0.15×** | **$12.99 @ 0.10×** | **$14.99 @ 0.10×** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **10,000** | Week-1 units | **1,500** | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| | Year-1 net to dev | **$33,147** | $28,734 | **$33,158** |
| **25,000** | Week-1 units | **3,750** | 2,500 | 2,500 |
| | Year-1 net to dev | **$82,867** | $71,835 | **$82,895** |
| **50,000** | Week-1 units | **7,500** | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| | Year-1 net to dev | **$165,734** | $143,669 | **$165,789** |

**Read that table carefully, because it says three things.**

1. **$9.99 and $14.99 produce the same money.** Not similar — the same, to within 0.03%. The conversion penalty for crossing $10 almost exactly cancels the 50% higher margin. There is no revenue argument for either one over the other.
2. **$12.99 is strictly dominated by $14.99.** Both sit in the same ">$10" conversion bucket, so $12.99 buys you *zero* extra units relative to $14.99 while giving up $2 of margin on every one. It loses ~13% of net revenue for nothing. **If the decision is to go above $10, the correct price is $14.99, not $12.99.** This is the clearest finding in the brief.
3. **Since revenue is a tie, the tiebreaker is not revenue.** At $9.99 you get **50% more human beings** for the same money — 1,500 players instead of 1,000 per 10k wishlists.

**For a 1v1–1v3 versus game, 50% more bodies at identical revenue is the whole argument.** PANOPTICON does not work without a live opponent. Buying half again as many players at no cost is the closest thing to a free lunch in this decision.

### The honest case against $9.99

This is real and the developer should weigh it, because the expert consensus is *against* me.

- **Every analyst who has looked at this says indies underprice.** Chris Zukowski: *"pick a price that is comparable to other indie games and then charge 20% more. If people are really really excited about your game, charge 50% more than you think you should."* — [How To Market A Game, 2022-08-23](https://howtomarketagame.com/2022/08/23/4-tips-to-help-you-price-your-indie-game/). VG Insights: *"When looking at 2 of the most popular price tags for indie games – $4.99 and $9.99, a developer should at least consider the possibility of pricing the game at $14.99 or $19.99… You can sell half the copies and still make more money when you price them at $19.99 rather than $9.99."* — [VG Insights, 2020-07-18](https://vginsights.com/insights/article/should-developers-charge-more-for-an-indie-game). Zukowski also found that indie prices have risen only ~40% in 16 years while AAA rose ~400%, and that inflation alone would put the average indie at **$11.61** in 2022 dollars — [HTMAG, 2022-08-17](https://howtomarketagame.com/2022/08/17/are-indie-games-too-cheap/).
- **"A low price point may be signalling low quality"** — VG Insights, same article. A 1v1 asymmetric shooter at $9.99 next to $14.99 Ultimate Chicken Horse and $14.99 Pummel Party may read as the lesser product.
- **The conversion number I am leaning on is not a clean $9.99-vs-$14.99 comparison.** It is "all prices" (which includes a lot of $2–5 games that drag the ratio up) versus ">$10". The true penalty for $14.99 specifically is probably smaller than 0.15→0.10, which would tip the table toward $14.99. **This is the weakest joint in my argument and I want it flagged.**
- **Cheap did not save the cheap games.** Stick Fight is $4.99 with **265** concurrent players. Crab Game is free with 2,921. Meanwhile $14.99 Pummel Party has **1,096** and $14.99 Golf With Your Friends has **979**. Price is a second-order lever on population; content depth is the first-order one. Pricing at $9.99 will not save a game people finish in three hours — it will just collect less on the way down.
- **At the median outcome this decision is worth almost nothing.** The median paid Steam `Party Game` has **31 reviews** (§5d). If PANOPTICON lands at the median, the price difference is worth a few hundred dollars. The price only matters in the top decile — and in that scenario the population problem has already solved itself, which argues for $14.99.

### Why a 20% launch discount specifically

**20% is a hard threshold in Valve's system, not a round number.** *"Any discount set to 20% or greater will automatically trigger email notifications to players with your game on their wishlist"* — [Steamworks Discounting](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/discounts). At 19% you get no email; at 20% every wishlister gets one. Since a launch discount can *only* be configured before release and cannot exceed 40% or run under 7 days, and since Valve warns that *"releasing with a very high launch discount… undermines the value of your title"*, **20% for 7 days is the cheapest point that buys the email.**

$9.99 at 20% = **$7.99** — which is, not coincidentally, exactly where PEAK and Content Warning both landed.

### What would change the answer between now and launch

| If this becomes true by ~2027-01 | Then |
|---|---|
| **A bot / single-player mode ships** | **$14.99.** This is the highest-leverage change on the list. Bots sever the price↔population link entirely — the game still works at 3am with nobody online — and once population stops binding, the revenue tie breaks toward margin. |
| Wishlists tracking above ~25k with streamer pickup already happening | **$14.99.** Demand guarantees the population; take the margin. Note co-op/versus "crewlikes" wildly over-convert (below), so a healthy wishlist number understates the real outcome. |
| Wishlists under ~5,000 at 3 months out | **$9.99**, and consider the full 40% launch discount. A thin wishlist means population is the only problem. |
| Map count lands at 3–5 rather than 8–10 | **$9.99 hard**, possibly $7.99. Content-per-dollar drives the review score, the score drives the population. |
| Map count lands at 10+, with progression and cosmetics | **$14.99** becomes defensible on content grounds. |
| Console port planned within 12 months | **$14.99.** Console storefronts anchor to the PC SKU; $9.99 on PC caps the console price. |
| **Under no circumstance** | **$12.99** — dominated by $14.99, see above. And never a round $12.00: **98.9%** of paid multiplayer games on Steam end in `.99` (§2). |

**One genuinely encouraging data point.** GameDiscoverCo's list of games that converted **at least 6× better than their wishlist balance predicted** is dominated by exactly this genre: **PEAK at 266×, Mage Arena 78×, R.E.P.O. 68×, Webfishing 67×** — *"there's a lot of online co-op centric crewlikes in here."* — [GDCo 2025-10-17](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-state-of-steam-wishlist-conversions). For friend-group multiplayer games the wishlist balance is a **floor, not a centre**, because purchases come from friends and streamer audiences who never wishlisted. The variance in this genre is enormous and skewed upward.

## 2. THE PRICE-POINT CLUSTERING QUESTION

**Short answer: yes, clustering is real, it is large, and I measured it directly rather than taking anyone's word for it.**

### 2a. Method

I did two things: I recovered the secondary literature, and — because none of it contains the histogram the question actually asks for — I computed the distribution myself.

**What the literature does and does not contain.** No source I could reach publishes a per-price-point histogram (% of games at exactly $4.99 vs $9.99 vs $14.99 vs $19.99). VG Insights and Chris Zukowski both publish *bucket* data, and their charts hold the finer numbers as images that are not machine-readable. So the exact clustering question was **NO SOURCE FOUND in the literature** — which is why I pulled the distribution. On 2026-09-09 I downloaded the full SteamSpy tag corpus for five tags via `https://steamspy.com/api.php?request=tag&tag=<TAG>` and computed the price distributions below myself. **n = 11,836 games for `Multiplayer` alone.** This is the strongest evidence in this brief and it is first-hand.

**Caveats, stated up front and honestly:**
- SteamSpy's `initialprice` field is the **current non-discounted list price**, not the historical launch price. So this is a snapshot of *what multiplayer games cost today*, not *what they launched at*. For a pricing decision being made today that is arguably the more relevant question, but it is not the same question.
- SteamSpy tracks a subset of Steam and its coverage of tiny games is incomplete; the true long tail of $0.99 shovelware is probably under-counted, which if anything **understates** the low end.
- There is no release-year filter available on this endpoint, so this mixes a 2014 game with a 2026 one.

### 2c. What the data actually says

**1. Charm pricing is not a convention, it is close to a law.** Of 9,161 paid Multiplayer games, **9,060 (98.9%) have a list price ending in `.99`.** 28 end in `.00`. 73 end in anything else. Across all five tags the figure is 98.5–99.1%. **Do not price PANOPTICON at $12.00 or $10.00.** The developer's stated instinct of "$12" should become **$11.99 or $12.99** if that neighbourhood is chosen at all — a round dollar price would put the game in the 0.3% of the market that does this, for no benefit I can find.

**2. The four canonical points really do dominate.** $4.99 + $9.99 + $14.99 + $19.99 alone account for **46.0% of all paid Multiplayer games** — four numbers out of a continuum, covering nearly half the market. Add $0.99, $2.99 and $1.99 and seven numbers cover **60.1%** of the market.

**3. $12.99 is a real but distinctly minority choice.** It is not exotic — 183 Multiplayer games and 16 Party Game titles use it — but it is **rank 15 of all price points in `Multiplayer`, 6.7× rarer than $9.99 and 4.4× rarer than $14.99.** In `Party Game` it does better relatively (rank 9, 3.9%) but on a tiny base of 16 games. **[Duck Game](https://store.steampowered.com/app/312530/) at $12.99 is the notable success at this price** — Overwhelmingly Positive, 39,050 reviews, 2–5M owners — so the price is clearly survivable.

**4. Party games are the cheapest multiplayer subgenre I measured.** Median paid `Party Game` list price is **$8.99**, versus $9.99 for `Multiplayer` and `Local Multiplayer`, and the *mean* is $10.15 versus $14.18 for `Multiplayer` — i.e. the party-game category lacks the expensive tail that drags the general multiplayer mean up. $4.99 is the **most common** party-game price (18.1%). If PANOPTICON is read by buyers as a party game, $14.99 puts it in the top ~25% of its category by price.

**5. On "does a higher price make more money" — the data is confounded and I will not pretend otherwise.** Median SteamSpy owners rise monotonically with price bucket in every tag I measured. For `Multiplayer`:

Read literally this says "charge more, sell more units *and* more revenue per unit." **That reading is almost certainly wrong.** Games that cost $30 are made by studios with money, marketing and QA; the price is a *symptom* of the budget, not the cause of the sales. Nothing here is a controlled experiment and I found no source that ran one. **What the table does legitimately establish is the negative claim: there is no evidence in this corpus that pricing low buys you units.** Anyone arguing "$9.99 will sell more copies than $14.99" is making an unsupported claim, and the population argument (§3) — not a units argument — is the honest reason to prefer $9.99.

**6. My own corpus *does* contain 183 of them, so "zero" is a sampling artifact — but two independent methods agreeing that the tier is hard to find is worth something. **$12.99 is a fully supported Valve price tier**, so its rarity is a market fact, not a platform restriction.

**7. The distribution evidence here is corroborating, not load-bearing.

## 3. THE MULTIPLAYER-SPECIFIC PRICING PROBLEM

### 3a. The tradeoff, stated correctly

The naive version of the argument is "price low so more people buy it, so lobbies fill." The data in §2c(5) does not support the first half of that sentence — there is no evidence that low prices buy units on Steam. **The correct version of the argument is narrower and survives the data:**

> A versus game's *value to a buyer* is a function of how many other people own it. Price does not reliably buy you units, but it does gate the specific marginal buyer who would try a weird indie game on a whim, and in a 1v1–1v3 game that marginal buyer *is the product*.

The supporting mechanism is documented: co-op games need a near-zero atomic network (*"For It Takes Two, you just need a buddy. That's it"*) whereas versus games need enough concurrent players with acceptable ping in your timezone to fill a lobby quickly — [GameDiscoverCo, "Analysis: multiplayer game discovery"](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/analysis-multiplayer-game-discovery).

**PANOPTICON's 1v1-to-1v3 target is a genuine structural advantage here and should be defended.** A game that needs 8 concurrent players to start has a much higher critical-mass threshold than one that works at 2. The already-completed session-structure brief in this directory makes the same point. If the game is playable and fun at 1v1, the population argument weakens considerably — and so does the argument for pricing low.

**The pattern worth noticing:** four asymmetric-multiplayer games in this set gave up on paid pricing entirely and went free — Deceit, Deceit 2, Unfortunate Spacemen, and Crab Game (free from day one). **None of them recovered a meaningful population.** Going free is not a rescue plan for this genre; it is what the rescue plan looks like after it has failed. Do not plan a price around the option to zero it later.

**PEAK and Content Warning are the two success cases, and both used a temporary near-zero launch price as an acquisition weapon while keeping the list price ~$8.** That is the strategy that actually worked twice, recently, in this exact genre neighbourhood — and it is exactly what a 10–40% launch discount off a $9.99 list is a smaller version of.

## 4. REGIONAL PRICING AND DISCOUNTING

### 4a. Steam's discounting rules — authoritative, fetched from Steamworks on 2026-09-09

All of the following is verbatim policy from [Steamworks → Sales and Marketing → Discounting](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/discounts). This section is fully sourced.

| Rule | The actual policy |
|---|---|
| **30-Day Release Cooldown** | "A product cannot be discounted for 30 days **following** its release, including both its Early Access release (if applicable) and its Full Release." **One exception: a Launch Discount.** |
| **Launch Discount** | "an optional introductory offer which allows a product to be on discount at its release. Launch Discounts can only be set up **prior to** product's release date, not after." |
| **Launch discount size** | **Minimum 10%, maximum 40%.** (Standard post-launch discounts: min 10%, max 95%.) |
| **Launch discount duration** | **Minimum 7 days, maximum 14 days.** (Standard discounts: min 1 day, max 14 days.) |
| **30-Day Price Increase Cooldown** | "A product cannot be discounted for 30 days following a price increase in **any** currency. There are no exceptions." Price *decreases* generate no cooldown. |
| **30-Day Discount Cooldown** | "A product cannot be discounted within 30 days of another discount." **Exception: the four Seasonal Sales** (Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter) are exempt from other discounts' cooldowns and generate none of their own — but a product on a Release Cooldown or Price Increase Cooldown still cannot join one. |
| **Base price changes during a discount** | "It's not possible to increase or decrease the base price of a product during an active discount, **or while a discount is scheduled in the future.**" |
| **Wishlist notification threshold** | "Any discount set to **20% or greater** will automatically trigger email notifications to players with your game on their wishlist." Also requires the discount run **over 8 hours** and affect the lowest-priced package. — [Wishlists doc](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/wishlist) |
| **Price changes near launch** | "To protect user expectations about a newly released game, we ask that price changes wait until **after 30 days from release**." — [Pricing doc](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/pricing) |

**The rule that matters most for PANOPTICON, quoted in full:**

> "It's typically best to ease into discounting, and increase the level of discount over time. For example, a title might move from **33% to 50% to 66% to 75%** and beyond over the course of a year or more. **Rushing into a 50% or 75% discount weeks after your launch, or releasing with a very high launch discount, sends a bad message to customers who bought at full price and undermines the value of your title.**" — [Steamworks Discounting](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/discounts)

Valve also says explicitly: *"it can be tempting to use small discounts in an attempt to climb the Top Sellers list or gain more exposure in the early days of your title, but look carefully at how many customers are purchasing your product at full price before entering a discount… it's probably best to use discounts once you've settled into the tail of your sales curve."*

**Read together, Valve's own guidance argues for a *modest* launch discount, not a big one.** That is why the recommendation above says 20% — the minimum that triggers wishlist emails — and not 30–40%.

### 4b. The launch-discount decision, concretely

- **You can only set a launch discount before release. There is no second chance.** If PANOPTICON ships 2027-04-01 with no launch discount, it then cannot be discounted at all until 2027-05-01.
- **A launch discount of ≥20% triggers wishlist emails to everyone who wishlisted the game.** Below 20%, it does not. **This is a hard threshold and it is the single most important number in this section.** If wishlist volume is meaningful, a 20% launch discount converts a passive wishlist into an active email campaign; a 15% one does not.
- **Counter-consideration:** a launch discount ≥20% is also the discount Valve explicitly warns "undermines the value of your title." And every wishlisted user *already* gets a release notification at launch regardless of discount ("any user that has the game on their wishlist at that time will receive an e-mail and/or mobile push notification about the game being available") — so the 20% discount email is a *second* touch, not the only one.
- **INFERENCE (mine):** the real choice is between **10–15%** (a courtesy discount that preserves price integrity but buys no email) and **exactly 20%** (buys the wishlist email blast at the lowest possible cost per unit). There is little reason to pick 25% or 30% — you pay more per unit for the same email, and Valve warns that deep launch discounts undermine the title's value. **The recommendation lands on 20%.** **NO SOURCE FOUND** for data comparing launch-discount sizes on outcomes (see §6f); Valve's advice above is qualitative.

### 4c. Discount cadence for a small multiplayer game

Valve's stated ladder is **33% → 50% → 66% → 75%** over a year or more, tied to content updates, with all editions/DLC discounted together.

**Valve's own measured justification for laddering, from its wishlist analysis:** *"games that 'stair-step' their discounts see a **70% increase in conversions** as compared to games that run the same discount percentage continually over time."* And: *"if a customer doesn't convert at a discount of 25% in May, they're unlikely to convert at that same 25% discount in June (all else equal)."* — [Valve, "Steam Platform Analysis: Wishlist Update"](https://steamcommunity.com/groups/steamworks/announcements/detail/950630846501680764). **This is a real number attached to the laddering advice and it is Valve's own.**

**The 2026–2027 seasonal sale calendar, from [Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/discounts/seasonalsales):**

| Sale | Dates |
|---|---|
| Autumn Sale 2026 | **Oct 1–8, 2026** |
| Winter Sale 2026 | **Dec 17, 2026 – Jan 4, 2027** |
| Spring Sale 2027 | **Mar 18–25, 2027** |
| Summer Sale 2027 | **Jun 24 – Jul 8, 2027** |

Steam Next Fest dates listed on Steamworks: **October 2026, February 2027, June 2027** (exact days INCOMPLETE) — [event calendar](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/event_calendar).

**Applied to a 2027-04-01 launch, this calendar has one sharp consequence:**

| Window | Action | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| **Mar 18–25, 2027** | **Spring Sale happens one week before launch.** The game is not out, so it cannot join — but it *can* be featured in adjacent Coming-Soon placements | Not a discount opportunity |
| **2027-04-01** | Launch + **20% launch discount, 7 days** → $7.99 | Launch discounts must be configured **before** release; 10–40%, 7–14 days |
| 2027-04-01 → 2027-05-01 | **No discounts possible at all** | 30-Day Release Cooldown |
| **2027-06-24 → 07-08** | **Summer Sale — the first seasonal the game can join.** ~12 weeks post-launch. Go ~20% | Seasonal sales are cooldown-exempt in both directions |
| Autumn Sale 2027 | ~33%, ideally on a map update | Seasonal exemption |
| Winter Sale 2027 | ~40–50% | Seasonal exemption |
| Between seasonals | One custom discount per 30 days, 1–14 days each | 30-Day Discount Cooldown |

**Because seasonal sales are exempt from the 30-day cooldown in both directions, all four are effectively free — take all four.** Custom discounts are the ones that cost scheduling flexibility.

**A documented workaround worth knowing:** the 30-day release blackout is not absolute in practice. Chris Zukowski, on Parcel Simulator, which launched 6 days before the Summer Sale: *"Usually you can't do a Steam sale within 30 days of your launch. **Unless you ask for it. So always ask for it using the support tool.**"* The game ran a discount June 20 → July 10. — [HTMAG, 2025-08-26](https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/08/26/the-demo-effect-from-7000-wishlists-to-42000/)

**A trap specific to this game's plan.** If the plan is to launch at $9.99 and raise later, note that **a price increase creates a 30-day discount cooldown and cannot be done during, or while scheduled near, any discount.** A raise must be slotted into a clear 30-day window that does not collide with a seasonal sale. Valve's own worked example in the Discounting doc exists because developers get this wrong.

**Cadence data specific to small multiplayer games: NO SOURCE FOUND.** General cadence guidance from [GameDiscoverCo, 2021-01-07](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/discount-strategy-your-key-post-launch): *"you should discount your games at every available opportunity… It's not seen as a negative from a player perspective, since most players only notice a fraction of available discounts"*, and *"gradually increasing discounts is the best practice. (So don't jump discounts down and then back up again.)"* Their worked case, Gibbous: A Cthulhu Adventure — 31,000 wishlists at launch, 40k lifetime units, a 70%-off Winter Sale did *"an average of 450 units sold per day"*; *"it took us ~1 year to reach 50% off."*

**Correction to a premise in the brief.** There is **no Steamworks rule that a discount must be below the price charged in the previous 30 days.** The real constraints are the four 30-day rules above plus the no-edit-during-discount rule. (The EU Omnibus Directive has a "lowest price in the prior 30 days" *display* requirement for e-commerce generally, but **NO SOURCE FOUND** tying it to Steamworks partner rules — do not assert it.)

**Converting reviews to units.** The standard method is the "Boxleiter number" — multiply reviews by a factor to estimate sales. **I could not source a current, credible value for that multiplier this session (the analyst writing on it — GameDiscoverCo, VG Insights — was in the research lane that failed). NO SOURCE FOUND.** The multiplier is also known to vary enormously by price, genre, era and region, which is exactly why the number should not be guessed. **Do not multiply the review percentiles above by any factor you have not sourced.**

**What this means for PANOPTICON, honestly:** the realistic planning question is not "$9.99 or $14.99" — at the median outcome for this genre, the price difference is worth a few hundred dollars, because the median outcome is a few hundred units. **The price decision only becomes financially material in the top decile of outcomes.** That argues, weakly, for pricing toward revenue-per-unit ($14.99) — because in the scenario where the price matters at all, the game is succeeding and the population argument has already been won. It argues the other way only if the low price is what *causes* entry into that top decile, and §2c(5) and §3b together find no evidence that it does.

**That is the strongest honest argument against my own recommendation, and it should be weighed.**

Valve's wishlist analysis — the post its current Wishlists doc still links to — **was recovered**. It is [**"Steam Platform Analysis: Wishlist Update", Steamworks Developer Group, posted 2016-03-29 (updated 2016-06-27)**](https://steamcommunity.com/groups/steamworks/announcements/detail/950630846501680764). **It is ten years old — treat as directional, not current.** Verbatim:

- **Email conversion:** *"Across Steam, we typically see an average of **3.4%** (median of 2.6%) of customers converting within a week of receiving an e-mail notification about a game on their wishlist."* (DLC: average 5.0%, median 3.0%.)
- **Lifetime conversion:** *"On average, games see **23% of their lifetime wishlist totals** converting to sales or activations."*
- **Where the conversions come from:** *"On average, **63%** of a game's wishlist conversions come from purchases at discount."*
- **Timing:** *"customers take **116 days** to purchase a game once added to their wishlist… Looking at the median conversion rate, we see customers converting only **36 days** after adding… we observe **31.4% of converting customers obtaining a game within one week** of adding it to their wishlist."*
- **Stair-stepping:** *"games that 'stair-step' their discounts see a **70% increase in conversions**"* vs running the same discount continually.
- **Email opt-out rate:** *"less than 1%."* And *"wishlists will never fully deplete."*

Valve's *current* doc explicitly refuses to give a launch formula: *"Because everyone wishlists for different reasons, there's no formula to accurately predict sales from wishlists."* — [Wishlists](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/wishlist)

### 6b. Launch-window conversion — the number people actually mean

Metric: (Week-1 total sales, wishlist conversions **plus** organic) ÷ (wishlist balance at launch).

| Cohort | Median ratio | Source |
|---|---|---|
| All games (survey, 100+ games launched since 2020) | **0.20×** | [GDCo, 2024-04-15](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/revealed-the-state-of-steam-wishlist) |
| Games launching with >10k wishlists | **0.17×** | same |
| Games launching with >25k wishlists (GDCo's own estimated data, all Steam) | **0.15×** | [GDCo, 2025-10-17](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-state-of-steam-wishlist-conversions) |
| …excluding adult/NSFW (a top-converting outlier group) | **0.14×** | same |
| **…games priced >$10 at launch** | **0.10×** | same |

GDCo's own framing of the variance: *"the performance range of 'wishlists at launch' compared to 'sales at the end of Week 1' varies by **10-20x, not 10-20%**."*

**Long tail multipliers** (same 2024 survey, and stable against their 2021 survey): **Month 1 = 1.39× Week 1** · **Year 1 gross = 3.16× Week 1** · **Cumulative Year 2 = 4.2× Week 1**.

**What separated the under-converters was reception, not wishlist count:** under-converters had a median Steam review score of **67% (Mixed)** after 7 days versus **91% (Very Positive)** for the top 20 Week-1 sellers.

### 6c. Does price affect wishlist conversion? Yes — and this is the finding

Verbatim, [GDCo 2025-10-17](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-state-of-steam-wishlist-conversions): *"we **know that very inexpensive games convert better**, and we didn't look at price. So if we sort only for games that cost **>$10 at launch**, we see a median drop all the way down to **0.10x**"* — against 0.14–0.15× for the all-price cohort. Concretely, GDCo: *"100,000 launch wishlist balance, 10k sales Week 1."*

**This is the only sourced price-bracket conversion split in existence that I could find. A finer bracket-by-bracket table: NO SOURCE FOUND.**

Two consequences, both load-bearing:
1. **The discontinuity is at $10, not at each price point.** $12.99 and $14.99 sit in the *same* bucket. So above $10 there is no conversion reason to take the lower number — which is what makes $12.99 dominated (see RECOMMENDATION).
2. **The penalty roughly cancels the margin gain.** $9.99 × 0.15 and $14.99 × 0.10 differ by 0.03% in revenue. The choice is therefore not about money.

**Caveat I want on the record:** the ">$10" cohort is compared against an *all-price* cohort that includes many $2–5 games. It is not a clean $9.99-vs-$14.99 test, and the true penalty at $14.99 specifically is probably smaller than 0.15→0.10. That would tip the calculation toward $14.99. **This is the weakest joint in the recommendation.**

### 6d. Multiplayer specifically — a large, favourable effect

GDCo's list of games converting **at least 6× better than expected** was dominated by online co-op:

| Game | Conversion vs expected |
|---|---|
| **PEAK** | **266×** |
| Mage Arena | 78× |
| **R.E.P.O.** | **68×** |
| Webfishing | 67× |
| FlyKnight | 18× |

GDCo's read, verbatim: *"if there's a trend here, it's — obviously — 'game went extremely viral with influencers and friend groups'. There's a **lot of online co-op centric crewlikes** in here."*

**INFERENCE (mine, not a source's):** for friend-group multiplayer games the wishlist balance systematically *understates* launch demand, because purchases come from friends and streamer audiences who never wishlisted. A wishlist-based forecast is a **floor, not a centre**, for this genre — and the upside variance is enormous.

### 6e. How many wishlists do you need?

From [Chris Zukowski, "Do wishlists matter any more?", 2024-01-29](https://howtomarketagame.com/2024/01/29/do-wishlists-matter-any-more/):

- **Valve (Erik Peterson, Steamworks talk), quoted:** *"Wishlists. Do they have an impact on your visibility on Steam? No! With a big asterisks"* — the asterisk being *"the Popular Upcoming section, that is affected by the number of wishlists that you have."*
- **Valve on magic numbers, quoted:** *"I've actually talked to developers all over the world that claim… maybe it's 50,000 or a 100,000 wishlists. I'm here to tell you that that's just simply not the case."*
- **Zukowski's rule of thumb (ANALYST):** *"I and many others have advised trying to get about **7,000–10,000 minimum wishlists before launch**"* — the threshold for Popular Upcoming, which is itself worth **~750–2,000 wishlists/day** while you are in it.
- **Why publishers ask for 30,000+:** a cushion against a bad conversion rate. His worked example: 7,000 WL × 3% × $19 = $3,990 vs 30,000 WL × 3% × $19 = $17,100.
- **Observed conversion range (ANALYST):** *"we don't know if your game is going to convert at 1%, 3%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 40% (yes all of those conversions are possible, I have seen the data for lots of games)."*
- **Velocity beats total:** divide wishlists by years on the store — **>15,000/year = good chance of being hot; <4,000/year = softer support.** GDCo agrees: *"What does appear to give you better predictability at Steam launch is follower or wishlist velocity directly before launch."*
- **Wishlists do not go stale** — Cosmoteer's per-month conversion was flat regardless of wishlist age ([HTMAG, 2023-02-06](https://howtomarketagame.com/2023/02/06/how-cosmoteers-4-5-year-marketing-campaign-shows-that-wishlists-dont-get-stale/)).
- Valve, on visibility floors: *"Q: Is there a minimum number of wishlists my game must have before Steam starts showing my game to users? A: No."*

**Composite forecast rule** (arithmetic chaining the sources above — not itself a source):
> Week-1 units ≈ launch wishlist balance × **0.10** (if priced >$10) to **0.15** (if ≤$10) → Month-1 ≈ 1.39× Week 1 → Year-1 ≈ 3.16× Week 1 → Year-2 cumulative ≈ 4.2× Week 1. Lifetime wishlist conversion tends toward Valve's **23%**, about **63%** of it arriving on discount.

### 6f. Launch-discount efficacy — still NO DATASET FOUND

**NO SOURCE FOUND** for any quantitative study of launch-discount efficacy or a recommended launch-discount percentage. GDCo's ["How long should you discount your game for?" (2024-09-06)](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/how-long-should-you-discount-your) is paywalled past an intro that only concedes *"the length of discount periods is something we haven't seen quantified that much."*

The single anecdote available, from [Zukowski, 2025-12-15](https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/12/15/leftover-research-from-2025/): **Tower Factory** (solo dev, 16 months) *"didn't offer a launch discount (dev still unsure if that was a good or bad decision)"*; first discount was 20% in the Winter Sale with Valve featuring; **6.8% conversion in month 1, rising to 13.7% and 52,000 copies at one year.**

**So the 20%-launch-discount recommendation rests on the *rule* (20% is the wishlist-email threshold) and on Valve's stair-stepping data, not on measured launch-discount outcomes.** Stated plainly so it can be discounted appropriately.

### Corrections to premises in the original brief

Four premises the research asked me to assume turned out to be wrong or unsupported. **None of these should be carried forward.**

1. **STRAFTAT is not "a notably cheap paid 1v1 shooter" — it is free-to-play**, and has been at every checkable point from pre-release onward. It monetises via cosmetic DLC ($6.99 / $9.99). — [archived 2024-10-27](https://web.archive.org/web/20241027104743/https://store.steampowered.com/app/2386720/)
2. **Murder Miners does not appear to be free-to-play and may never have been.** It is $2.99 and `is_free: false` today; a scan of ~150 of its Steam news posts (2014–2026) found no F2P announcement.
3. **White Noise 2 is not free-to-play.** It launched at $9.99 and is $9.99 today.
4. **Dead by Daylight has no documented price increase.** ~19 Wayback snapshots sampled Oct 2018 → Aug 2026 show **$19.99 in every clean non-sale capture**. (A 27-month gap exists for Jun 2016 – Sep 2018 because Steam's age gate blocked all Wayback crawls, so a change inside that window cannot be strictly ruled out — but no evidence of one was found.)
5. **RUB was not removed from Steam.** It is still a live currency. **TRY and ARS** were removed in Nov 2023, replaced by the USD_MENA and USD_LATAM buckets.
6. **There is no Steamworks rule that a discount must beat the previous 30 days' price.** That is an EU Omnibus *display* requirement for e-commerce generally; **NO SOURCE FOUND** tying it to Steamworks partner rules.
7. A 2022 GameDiscoverCo post gives the price-increase discount lockout as **28 days**; Valve's current doc says **30**. Use 30.
