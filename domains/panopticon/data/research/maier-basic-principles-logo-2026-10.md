**Limits, stated plainly**
- I read the **captions and running text, not the plates**. Most pages are illustrations; the OCR of lettering plates
  is garbage, and I did not look at those images (one exception: p. 98 of vol. 2).
- Page numbers are the printed folios. Ones marked "~" sit on unnumbered front-matter pages and are inferred.
- Not used: the Scribd vol. 4 copy (login-walled), the separate per-volume archive.org items (`basicprincipleso000Nmaie`,
  lending-restricted, so not opened), Google Books previews (not needed once the full open scan was found), course readers.
- **What the book does not contain:** it has no section on logos or wordmarks, kerning tables, arched or circular
  baselines, letter-spacing as a typographic setting, or "reading speed". It is a teaching record that describes
  exercises, not a rulebook. Maier says so himself: "Principles of aesthetic composition are not offered and single,
  chance results are not goals" (vol. 4 p. 42). Everything in section 3 applies the book's principles; it is not
  something Maier wrote about logos.

## 2. Principles, with quotes

### 2.1 Lettering (vol. 2, Gürtler, pp. 76-104)
- Readability sits in the sequence, not the single letter: "The optical problem of readability involves the
  interdependence of the individual symbols and the harmonious sequence of the letters in a text" (v2 p. 76).
- The look of lettering comes from forms *and* gaps: "The appearance and formal unity of letterforms are determined
  by the interrelationships among basic forms of individual characters and spaces between characters and by the
  continuous, rhythmic properties of the strokes" (v2 p. 76).
- **Optical spacing is the core rule:** "A formal unity of word and line structure and a rhythmically balanced
  organization of the entire page are produced through the optical equality of interior and exterior spaces between
  characters and line intervals. The basic interval between two verticals is the starting point of the optical
  adjustment. The interval is somewhat smaller than a square. Additional spaces produced with diagonals or half
  verticals must be optically equal to the basic interval" (v2 p. 77).
- Spacing is set by eye, without guides: lines "should not be influenced by helping lines" (v2 p. 77).
- Unequal spacing reads as broken rhythm: the enlarged detail "shows unrhythmic spacing. The spaces are optically
  unequal" (v2 p. 81). The aim is "a balanced unity of quantity, organization, and rhythm" (v2 p. 81).
- Stroke character: the stroke "must be lively and exciting. Accentuating the ends makes its form more distinct"
  (v2 p. 77). Pressure changes must not be abrupt and should flow evenly (v2 p. 77).
- Curves: "The curve is fundamentally symmetrical and wider than the geometric half circle" (v2 p. 82).
- **Letter-specific optical corrections (capitals):** forms are sized by how open they are: "the letter O is small
  because of its closed nature; the termination of the letter N ascends slightly to the right and is compensated for
  by its two pointed angles" (v2 p. 85). If the bowls of B and P are too narrow, "the forms appear narrow because of
  this incorrect proportioning" (v2 p. 85). The R curve should be "slightly elevated", not pulled down; the K
  vertical slightly taller than cap height; the Y junction "just below the geometric center" (v2 p. 85).
- In the capital-text exercise, word spaces are dropped so that the student can concentrate on the surface: "Intervals between words are not considered in order to
  concentrate on organizing the lettering surface" (v2 p. 85). This makes the line of capitals into an even texture.
- Contrast within strokes: "The contrasting rhythm of thick and thin line segments within the individual letters
  produces a dynamic quality" (v2 p. 90). With less thick-thin contrast (pen held at a diagonal) the letters "appear
  more fluid in comparison to the preceding examples with stiff forms" (v2 p. 100).
- Colour of the writing: black ink "mixed equally with water to obtain a lighter line. A color relationship between
  the writing and the surface is produced by using absorbent, yellow-toned paper" (v2 p. 90).
- Weight from tool: too soft a pencil gives "a broad, awkward stroke"; too hard "impedes clear modulation" (v2 p. 77).
- Composing with sign-like elements: "Optically adjusted placement of neutral signs and rhythmically balanced
  organization of the surface" (v2 p. 96). Uppercase text: "optical and rhythmical organization" (v2 p. 98, from the page image).
- Lowercase is easier to read as words because it depends on "connections between the characters, organization of
  the text into separate words". Capitals are "individual, unconnected forms" (v2 p. 102).

### 2.2 Interval, proportion, legibility (vol. 4, Maier, Graphic Exercises, pp. 42-73)
- **Fusion destroys the form:** four black strips butted together make "a black quadratic shape is produced, and they
  are no longer readable as a graphic form" (v4 p. 43).
- **Extreme spreading reads as gaps, not form:** "Extreme situations are produced that are legible only in a
  relationship ... the fifth example in the second row can be read only as line intervals" (v4 p. 46).
- The criteria pairs Maier uses to sort variations are light-dark, static-dynamic, simple-complex, one-several and few-many
  (v4 p. 48). Flat-oblique is added at p. 50.
- Smallest unit of proportion is three: "piece 1 is to piece 2 as piece 2 is to piece 3 and to the totality" (v4 p. 49).
- Quality comes from relating parts, not adding: "Imperfections may not be reduced adding pieces (quantity): quality
  must be achieved by relating the existing pieces to each other and to the whole" (v4 p. 49).
- Density changes how a sequence reads. Contrast was "reduced by increasing the number and density of the formal element"
  (v4 p. 64).
- Ten equal lines at regular intervals "form a gray value inside the format" (v4 p. 68). Even rhythm reads as a tone.

### 2.3 Colour and value (vol. 4 Color 1; vol. 3 Color 2; vol. 2 Memory Drawing)
- "The appearance of a color cannot be studied in isolation: it must be seen in relation to other colors" (v4 p. 11).
  Colours differ in three ways: hue, value and brightness (saturation) (v4 pp. 11-12).
- Simultaneous contrast: "A bordering color can influence it in color direction (yellower, blue, redder), value
  (lighter, darker), and brightness" (v4 p. 22).
- Quantity contrast: proportions can strengthen or weaken an existing contrast, and "A change of sequence also produces a weight shift"
  (v4 p. 30).
- "The use of white as a color must be arranged harmoniously in a dark environment". In the same passage a hard light area
  "would disturb the entire composition", and repeating light areas in graded sizes "produces a dynamic effect" (v2 p. 39).
- The same colour sketch looks different when enlarged: "the effect of the same color is different because of the
  quantity change in the enlargement" (v2 p. ~11).
- Opposites such as "light-heavy, quiet-active" are translated into colour (v3 p. 85). The light/dark reading can
  flip with lighting and surface (v3 p. 18).

### 2.4 Format, placement, figure-ground (vol. 1; vol. 2 perspective; vol. 3)
- "The representation should be interestingly placed within in the format, and inessential elements omitted" (v1 p. 70).
- Scale against the frame changes the mood. A drawing that "makes full use of the paper format" is "delicate, light ...
  conceptually restrained" (v1 p. 84). When "the format of the paper is almost overpowered", the study is "expressive,
  dynamic" (v1 p. 85).
- Negative space is a shape: "negative spaces are drawn as shapes. They clarify the structure" (v1 p. 28). Students aim
  for "the harmonious interrelationship between forms and negative spaces" (v1 p. 79). Counterform appears as a cast
  in Dimensional Design (v4 p. 75).
- Contrast pairs listed: "large-small, high-low, narrow-wide, curved-straight, light-dark" (v1 p. 74) and "light-dark,
  large-small, linear-surface" (v2 p. ~11).
- Orientation changes expression: "the picture can be turned to change the space and the expression" (v2 p. 61). One of the
  recommended views is "normal view, slightly asymmetrical" (v2 p. 60). A diagonal tilt is held in check by an opposing
  movement: "The dominant diagonal is balanced by the counter-movement of the threads" (v3 p. 35).
- Required asymmetry in a progression: "The sequence must also contain an asymmetrical progression" (v4 p. 32).

## 3. Applying this to Ryan's choices

"Says" = stated in the book. "Implies" = my application of a stated principle (Maier does not discuss logos).

| Choice | What Maier's principles do to reading/perception | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Letter-spacing: fused** | Implies a solid mass that stops reading as letters. Fused strips become "a black quadratic shape ... no longer readable as a graphic form". In cream on dark, PANOPTICON would turn into a bar. | v4 p. 43 |
| **Letter-spacing: normal to slightly open** | Says unity and balanced rhythm come from interior and exterior spaces being optically equal. The base interval between two verticals is "somewhat smaller than a square". Implies the calmest and fastest-reading setting: the gaps match the counters, so the word reads as one even grey tone. | v2 p. 77, v2 p. 76 |
| **Letter-spacing: very wide** | Implies the eye reads the gaps, not the word: extremes are "legible only in a relationship ... read only as line intervals". Letters read one at a time and slower, as if spelled out. The word looks more monumental and quiet, but loses its unity as a word. Capitals are already "individual, unconnected forms", so wide tracking makes this worse. Choose it only if the slow, spelled-out read is wanted, and keep each gap optically equal. | v4 p. 46, v2 p. 102 |
| **Optical vs mechanical spacing** | Says optical, without question: spaces from diagonals or half-verticals "must be optically equal to the basic interval", set "without helping lines". Unequal optical spaces read as "unrhythmic". PANOPTICON's trouble spots are A (diagonals), the open sides of P and C, T (big side spaces) and the O–P and O–N joins. Mechanical spacing will leave holes at P-A, A-N, T-I and C-O. | v2 pp. 77, 81 |
| **Letter proportion and optical size** | Says O is drawn smaller because it is closed, so in practice the O should overshoot so it does not look small. N's rising right end is balanced by its points. If P bowls are too narrow, "the forms appear narrow". Check IM Fell's two Ps: their bowls set how wide the whole word feels. | v2 p. 85 |
| **Weight** | Says thick-thin contrast "produces a dynamic quality"; lower contrast reads "more fluid"; a stroke that is too heavy is "broad, awkward" and kills modulation. Implies keeping IM Fell's regular weight, which already has a clear old-style modulation. Faux-bolding or a heavy stroke fills the counters and closes the intervals, which pushes the word toward the fused, unreadable end. Light weight on dark loses the stroke "accent". | v2 pp. 77, 90, 100 |
| **One line vs stacked (PANOP / TICON)** | Says readability comes from "the harmonious sequence of the letters". Implies a one-line setting reads as one word and one unit. Stacking breaks that sequence into two units, PANOP and TICON, which read as two pieces of a word, slower and more like a puzzle. If stacked, Maier's rule for line spacing applies: the space between lines must be optically equal to the space between letters, with "clear separations between the lines". The two lines also differ in mass (two Ps and an A against T, I, C), so they need balancing. | v2 pp. 76, 77, 102 |
| **Scale in the frame** | Says a word that fills the format calmly reads "delicate ... restrained", while one that nearly "overpowers" the format reads "expressive, dynamic". It should be "interestingly placed", with anything inessential left out. Implies a mid scale with generous dark field for calm, archival authority, and edge-to-edge for aggression. Says the same colour behaves differently at different sizes (quantity), so check the cream at final size. | v1 pp. 70, 84-85; v2 p. ~11 |
| **Baseline: straight / arched / circular** | Maier does not discuss curved baselines. By extension: a straight horizontal reads as static and calm, while a tilt or curve adds direction and movement, and Maier pairs a dominant direction with a "counter-movement" to keep balance. Turning the field changes "the space and the expression". An arch or circle also turns every gap into a wedge, so the optical-equality rule (v2 p. 77) becomes much harder to meet. A circle around a central device would echo the panopticon plan (my inference, not Maier's). | v4 p. 48 (static-dynamic), v3 p. 35, v2 p. 61 |
| **Colour and value (cream on dark)** | Says colour is only judged against its neighbour. The dark ground will shift the cream in hue, value and brightness (simultaneous contrast), so test it on the real ground. The lettering course itself used softened ink on "yellow-toned paper" to get "a color relationship between the writing and the surface". That supports a slightly lowered contrast (dingy cream rather than white) as a deliberate unifier. Light colour in a dark field must be "arranged harmoniously"; a hard light area "would disturb the entire composition". Implies avoiding pure white, keeping the cream warm and slightly greyed, and remembering that the thin strokes lose value first at small sizes. | v4 pp. 11, 22, 30; v2 pp. 39, 90 |
| **Symmetry (PANOP\|TICON; repeated P, O, N)** | Says the curve itself is symmetrical, but Maier's sequences prize an "asymmetrical progression" and a "slightly asymmetrical" normal view. The word is not mirror-symmetric: P-A-N-O-P against T-I-C-O-N. The repeats (P at 1 and 5, O at 4 and 9, N at 3 and 10) give a built-in rhythm of returns. Maier's three-part proportion (1:2 as 2:3 and to the whole) is the tool for judging it. Implies spacing it as one even rhythm, not forcing a centre gap or mirror. Let the repeated O and N set the beat, and make the two Ps and the two Os identical in optical size so the repeats read as rhythm, not accident. | v2 p. 82, v4 pp. 32, 49, v2 p. 60 |
| **Texture / density** | Says lines at regular intervals read as "a gray value", and adding density lowers contrast. Implies that an evenly spaced wordmark reads as one tone (calm, institutional), while uneven density draws the eye to the dense spot. | v4 pp. 64, 68 |

## 4. Short version
1. Space by eye. Make every gap optically equal to the gap between two verticals, a little less than a square (v2 p. 77).
2. Avoid both extremes. Fused spacing becomes a block (v4 p. 43), and very wide spacing reads as gaps rather than a word (v4 p. 46).
3. Draw O and other closed rounds optically larger, and keep the P bowls full width or the word looks narrow (v2 p. 85).
4. Keep IM Fell's thick-thin contrast, which is what makes it look alive (v2 p. 90). Do not thicken it until the counters close (v2 p. 77).
5. Keep one line for one reading. If it must stack, match the line gap to the letter gap (v2 pp. 76-77, 102).
6. Scale sets the mood: generous dark field for restraint, nearly filled frame for force (v1 pp. 84-85).
7. Judge the cream only on the actual dark ground and at the actual size (v4 pp. 11, 22, 30; v2 p. ~11). A warm, lowered cream follows Maier's own yellow-paper, diluted-ink pairing (v2 p. 90).
8. Keep it straight unless movement is wanted. A curve adds dynamism and makes optical spacing harder (inference from v4 p. 48, v3 p. 35, v2 p. 77).
