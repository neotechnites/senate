Part two: identifying the four key gameplay elements. When shoppers are arriving on your Steam page for the first time, they have a very important question that they're asking themselves: what do you do in this game? It sounds obvious. You've been working on this game for a long time. But I find that a lot of developers actually have a problem identifying what's in their game. You've been so close to it, it's really hard to step back and just give a short four-part answer. So I'm going to help you in this lecture.

In game development circles, this is called the core gameplay loop. The core gameplay loop is just what you do over and over again, and it's the core center of your game. For example, let's say you're going to do the core gameplay loop, or the four key elements, for a game like Breath of the Wild. It's a sprawling big game, and there's a lot of neat things in that game, but I would break the four key elements down to this: you explore the vast world; you solve puzzles in these little temples; you do big boss fights; and then you do cooking and crafting with all the things you find when you're doing that exploration. Those are the four key gameplay elements.

For Rogue Legacy, let's say you were making Rogue Legacy, not just setting it as your target game. I would say your four key gameplay elements are fighting enemies, selecting the next person in the lineage, exploring the castle, and then buying upgrades. So what we're going to do is take those four key gameplay elements and put them in the spreadsheet that I've linked below, on this special tab called Your Game. You've got gameplay element one, gameplay element two, gameplay element three, and four, and you just put that in there: fighting bosses, selecting the next person in your lineage, exploring the castle, and upgrading your town, buying upgrades. You've got to do this for your game.

It might be hard to do this. I recommend just asking a friend who's played your game, or really thinking back to the first things that you implemented in your game, because usually those are your core gameplay loop. All of those will help you identify what you do in this game, and it'll be very helpful when we get to the point where we're going to be picking our screenshots, doing our trailer, and also doing the long description called About This Game.

Another way to find similar games is to go to YouTube. On YouTube, a lot of people do the same thing that the Steam shoppers do, where they say "this game looks like this game and this game". So what I recommend is going to the YouTube trailers or Let's Plays for your target game, scrolling down into the comments, and looking for those comments. Here's one right here. This guy says, "I get the vibes of The Longest Journey or Syberia. Maybe the graphics. It's a nice looking game." There you go. Your target games are The Longest Journey and Syberia.

So why is it so important to know the target game and your four gameplay elements? Before you start building your page, you're going to have a lot of creative ideas for your Steam page, but you really need to boil it down to just these four. Otherwise, it's going to be hard to interpret your page when shoppers are looking at it. It also helps solidify the game for your team. Your team might be several people: an artist, a marketing person, the CEO, the creative designer of the whole thing. All of these people can have a slightly different idea of what the game is. But when you all agree on what the four key gameplay elements are, it helps solidify the critique of your game's store page, because you can keep saying, "did we cover these four gameplay elements?" That's why we do it. It helps everybody get on the same page.

This all might seem super obvious, and you might be tempted to skip over this section and start picking screenshots or something. But I find that the developers who understand their game better, and the other games in the market, will have a much easier time making their Steam page. It's going to make it so much better.
